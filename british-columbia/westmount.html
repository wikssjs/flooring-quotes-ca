Flooring options and costs in Westmount

Westmount homeowners typically have three favourites for everyday living—engineered hardwood, luxury vinyl plank (LVP), and tile—but the “best” choice depends on where in the home you’re installing it. With a population of 1,076 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), the local market is smaller than Vancouver, so dependable crews can book out faster during peak renovation months. Westmount’s older housing stock is also a big cost shaper: where a subfloor has worn plywood or uneven concrete, you often need levelling and preparation before any flooring goes down—commonly adding roughly $1–$3 per sq ft to the project total.

In the Lower Mainland–Southwest region, the wet, marine climate creates indoor humidity swings, especially in winter and shoulder seasons. Basements and slab-on-grade areas can show moisture wicking through concrete, which is why solid hardwood (even when installed correctly above grade) is generally avoided below grade. For main living areas, solid hardwood remains popular, but contractors usually specify proper expansion gaps and humidity control. Where radiant heating is present, options like engineered wood, LVP, tile, and temperature-rated laminates tend to fit best—while most solid hardwood needs very careful product and system matching.

Because Westmount is close to the busiest installer routes in the Lower Mainland, demand is especially strong around Westmount Village and the nearby residential streets where homeowners renovate kitchens, living rooms, and hallways. What affects the price of flooring in Westmount

In the Lower Mainland–Southwest region, it’s common for the same flooring type to come in 30–50% apart between quotes once you account for preparation, subfloor repairs, transitions, and waste. British Columbia pricing varies because labour availability is tighter around the metro corridor, and older homes can require more correction work than homeowners expect. The technical drivers are also more strict here: the Lower Mainland’s wet, marine climate brings indoor humidity swings, and below-grade areas can develop moisture vapour pressure through concrete. Solid hardwood expands and contracts with humidity, and it’s not a good match for basements or slab-on-grade moisture—so quotes that include an engineered hardwood or LVP solution can be more stable even when product choices differ.

Radiant heating is another pricing lever. In colder pockets and newer builds across the region, many homeowners want in-floor comfort, but that rules out most solid hardwood and some laminate products unless the flooring is explicitly rated for higher temperatures. You’ll also see cost differences from older regional housing stock: levelling, replacing soft sections, and moisture barrier upgrades frequently add about $1–$4 per sq ft depending on how much “repair before install” is needed.

Concrete examples we see in Westmount: (1) a living-room project over an uneven plywood subfloor where floor smoothing and patching is required can push an LVP job closer to the upper end of the $5–$12 per sq ft band; (2) a basement renovation where moisture vapour control is needed can make solid hardwood impractical, but engineered wood or LVP typically keeps the project within its expected range.

Permits & regulations in British Columbia

In British Columbia, installing floor covering itself usually does not require a building permit. That means carpet, laminate, engineered hardwood, LVP, and tile—when treated as finish work—are typically carried out without a permit. Where homeowners can get tripped up is when the “flooring job” includes work that touches building systems. For example, structural subfloor repair or replacing load-bearing components can require permits depending on scope, and in-floor heating circuit work (electrical connections, sensor wiring, or modifications to the electrical system) may trigger electrical permitting requirements.

In condominiums, you also have rules beyond permits. Many buildings restrict or require specific underlay and sound-transmission control to meet STC/IIC expectations. That can affect your choice of underlay under hardwood or tile on upper floors, and sometimes it requires board approval even if the installation itself wouldn’t trigger a permit.

To verify a contractor properly in Westmount and across BC, follow a simple checklist: (1) ask for their proof of business registration/contractor information as applicable for their trade scope; (2) request a certificate of insurance showing liability coverage; (3) confirm workers’ coverage (WSIB/WCB) for their employees; and (4) obtain a clearance letter if the contractor uses subcontract labour. If you’re hiring for in-floor heating or any electrical-adjacent work, ensure licensed electrical work is handled through the correct channels before the schedule starts. Then, for your condo, send a written request to the corporation/strata before you install—especially for hardwood and tile on upper levels.

Best flooring for each room in your Westmount home

Choosing the right flooring for each space in Westmount starts with how moisture behaves there. The Lower Mainland–Southwest climate brings indoor humidity swings, and basements or slab-on-grade areas can be vulnerable to moisture vapour coming through concrete. That’s why living and dining areas usually do best with engineered hardwood or high-quality laminate: they balance warmth and style while tolerating humidity changes better than solid hardwood. For bedrooms, you’ll often get the best comfort and sound dampening from carpet, but engineered hardwood is also a strong option if you have allergies or want easier cleaning.

In kitchens, the highest risk is spills and frequent wet mopping. Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) is a practical first choice because it’s waterproof and handles minor moisture events well; large-format porcelain tile is the other top performer for water resistance and durability. Basements (finished) are where the climate really decides: LVP is the clear winner because it’s waterproof and tolerates below-grade conditions far more reliably than solid wood. Bathrooms should be porcelain or ceramic tile only, with proper substrate prep and water management details to protect your investment. If you have radiant heating, many newer Westmount homes are prioritizing comfort there, and engineered wood, LVP, tile, and some rated laminates pair best—while solid hardwood often needs extra careful specification.

As a dollar example: switching a 400 sq ft main-floor living area from laminate (often in the $4–$10 per sq ft band) to engineered hardwood (commonly $10–$17 per sq ft) can mean a material difference of roughly $2,400–$5,200 on materials alone, but it’s often justified if you plan to stay long-term and want better feel and resale appeal in your main living areas.

How to choose a flooring contractor in Westmount

Finding the right flooring contractor in Westmount is mostly about verification and clarity. First, confirm British Columbia compliance through three checks: (1) licensing/registration where applicable for their scope (ask directly for their current business information and any trade credentials tied to their work); (2) liability insurance—request a certificate of insurance; and (3) workers’ coverage (WSIB/WCB) for employees. If you can’t get documentation before signing, treat that as a major warning. Next, protect yourself with quotes: ask for 2–3 written, itemised estimates that break labour and materials separately (including underlay, adhesives, levelling compounds, transition profiles, and stair work). A lump-sum number without scope detail is where disputes start.

Read the scope like a contract, not a brochure. Does the quote include subfloor levelling, patching, or moisture barrier prep? Is removal and disposal included? Are transitions and stair nosing priced per step or per linear foot? If you have radiant heat, ask whether the contractor confirms product suitability for the operating temperature profile. Warranty matters too: ask for the workmanship warranty length, the product/manufacturer warranty terms, and whether any warranty is transferable to a new homeowner if you sell.

Finally, manage payment responsibly. In most Westmount renovations, you should never pay more than about 10–15% upfront; hold back the remainder until the job is complete, cleaned, and inspected. Get a start date and completion estimate in writing, plus a plan for material lead times so you’re not stuck mid-renovation.

Frequently asked questions — flooring in Westmount

Can I install hardwood over concrete?

In most Westmount homes, you can, but the “how” matters. Solid hardwood generally should not be installed over concrete slabs in areas where moisture vapour is a concern, and this is especially important in the Lower Mainland–Southwest where marine humidity swings and basement moisture can affect concrete. If the concrete is on-grade and the moisture conditions are well controlled, engineered hardwood is typically the better choice because it’s more stable and can be specified for glue-down or floating installs with the right vapour management. If you’re considering a solid hardwood look, plan on above-grade installations only and a strict humidity-control approach. When budgeting, remember hardwood options commonly fall in the $9–$18 per sq ft band depending on product and prep needs.

How long does flooring installation take?

Timelines in British Columbia vary mainly by room count, subfloor prep, and how much demo/levelling is needed. A straightforward click-lock laminate job on one main-floor level can progress quickly, but in Westmount, many projects require at least some patching or smoothing because older plywood and uneven concrete are common. For example, if you’re installing 300–500 sq ft and the subfloor is already flat, the install may be done in a few days, with additional time for acclimation and finishing. If levelling and moisture barrier work are required, expect extra time before any materials go down. LVP and engineered hardwood often move faster than tile, while tile needs more time for curing. Always ask your contractor for start date and completion estimate in writing, especially with material lead times.

What flooring works with radiant heat?

Radiant heat compatibility is one of the key technical filters we use in Westmount. Engineered hardwood and LVP are commonly the best fits because their construction tolerates temperature changes better than most solid hardwood. Tile is also a reliable pairing since it handles heat well and provides excellent thermal transfer. Some laminates can work too, but only if the product is rated for radiant heat—don’t assume all click-lock laminates are suitable. If you have in-floor heating, ask your contractor to confirm the specific flooring product rating and install method (including underlay details where relevant). This is one reason quotes can vary: radiant-ready specs may push you toward the upper end of material bands (for instance, engineered hardwood often lands in the $10–$17 per sq ft range), but it reduces the risk of premature gapping or performance issues.

Engineered vs solid hardwood — what's the difference?

Solid hardwood is a single piece of wood strip/plank installed as nail-down, and it’s strongly affected by humidity because it’s not layered for dimensional stability. In British Columbia, that’s why we typically keep solid hardwood above grade and emphasize expansion gaps and indoor humidity control—especially in homes with older construction or basements where moisture vapour may occur. Engineered hardwood is layered (top wood veneer over stable core layers), so it tolerates humidity swings better and is typically the safer choice for on-grade and many above/below-adjacent scenarios when specified correctly. That stability is one reason engineered hardwood frequently earns a place in the Lower Mainland–Southwest market even when homeowners like the “real wood” look of solid. In pricing, both can be in the hardwood band, but engineered often competes well with the $9–$18 per sq ft range once prep is included.

Do I need to level my subfloor first?

In most Westmount installs, levelling is either required or at least strongly recommended—especially if you’re installing click-lock floors, engineered wood, or LVP where flatness affects performance and the look of seams. Subfloor levelling and repair commonly adds cost because the contractor may need grinding, patching, or smoothing compounds before any flooring starts. Regionally, older housing stock means uneven plywood or concrete surfaces are common, and that prep work often adds about $1–$4 per sq ft depending on severity. If the subfloor isn’t level, you can get raised edges, squeaks, gaps, or premature wear. The right approach depends on the flooring type and thickness, so ask for the contractor’s flatness method (what they measure and how they correct) before you approve your final scope.

What's the most durable flooring for pets and kids?

For pets and kids in Westmount, durability usually comes down to scratch resistance, dent resistance, and water tolerance. Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) is often the top practical pick because many waterproof products can handle spills quickly and recover visually better than materials that swell when exposed to moisture. If you want the longest service life and the easiest cleaning around accidents, porcelain/ceramic tile is extremely durable, though it’s harder underfoot and can be colder without area rugs. Carpet can be comfortable and forgiving, but it needs a dry subfloor and professional spot/cleaning practices to avoid matting and odour. In a wet, marine climate with indoor humidity swings and potential basement moisture, LVP’s tolerance makes it a common homeowner choice. Budget-wise, LVP typically lands in the $5.50–$12.00 per sq ft band, often delivering better day-to-day performance than lower-cost laminate ($4–$10 per sq ft) in active family homes.
