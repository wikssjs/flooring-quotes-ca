Flooring options and costs in Kingston

Flooring costs in Kingston often feel all over the map because the city’s housing mix is a big driver: with 52.7% of homes built before 1981 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), many projects start with subfloor patching, levelling, or moisture checks before a new surface can go down. In practice, that prep work can add real labour hours and can be the difference between a straightforward install and one that needs additional materials. Kingston also sits in a climate where indoor humidity can swing seasonally—especially in older homes where air sealing and ventilation weren’t built to today’s standards—so the “same” material can behave differently from year to year. It’s also why basement moisture risk matters in the Kingston–Pembroke region; below-grade spaces can’t be treated like “just another room.”

Because of that, contractors in Kingston tend to steer homeowners toward engineered wood and luxury vinyl plank (LVP) for most of the house, reserving solid hardwood for above-grade installs where the home can maintain steadier moisture conditions. In-floor heating is also common in upgrades, and that can narrow choices further because not every product is radiant-compatible. Demand is particularly steady in West End Kingston and around the Portsmouth neighbourhood, where many older houses are being refreshed with durable finishes for busy family life. Before you compare materials, it helps to understand the price band for supply and labour—then budget separately for site prep if your subfloor needs attention.

What affects the price of flooring in Kingston

In Kingston–Pembroke and across Ontario, quotes for the same “about 1,000 sq ft, same room layout” can vary by 30–50% because the cost is rarely just the floor covering. Labour and prep are the biggest swing factors—especially in older neighbourhoods where 1960s to 1970s subfloors are common, and where 52.7% of homes built before 1981 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census) often means you’ll see patching, old underlayment removal, or uneven boards before installation can start. When the contractor prices for those unknowns (flatness testing, moisture mitigation, additional underlayment or smoothing compound), your number rises—when the subfloor is already solid, the same project can come in much lower.

Technically, Kingston’s humidity swings and basement moisture risk matter. Solid hardwood expands and contracts with humidity, and it’s typically not glued or floated over concrete; solid wood in basements or on moisture-prone slabs performs poorly. Engineered hardwood and luxury vinyl plank handle moisture movement far better and are much more suitable for basement installs. Also, colder periods in Ontario push many buyers toward radiant-compatible flooring options where in-floor heat exists; that rules out a larger portion of solid hardwood and some laminate. Finally, older regional stock frequently needs levelling or repair, and that’s commonly where homeowners feel the dollars: adding even $1–$4 per sq ft to prep can change a project by several thousand dollars.

Concrete examples from local installs: replacing a damaged OSB section or patching a dip near an exterior wall can add labour the same day; finding a concrete slab that’s not flat can trigger extra self-levelling compound. On the other hand, a clean, flat subfloor in an above-grade room can keep costs closer to the lower end of the $4–$10 laminate/LVP band or the $11–$25 hardwood band.

Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, installing flooring itself generally does not require a building permit. That said, associated work can trigger permit requirements, especially when the project touches electrical systems or structural elements. For example, if you’re upgrading or adding in-floor heating circuits (radiant), that electrical work may require an electrical permit and a licensed electrician to complete it to code. Similarly, if the job includes structural subfloor repair beyond “like-for-like replacement”—such as significant reconstruction, load-bearing changes, or major moisture remediation that affects building assemblies—permits and inspections may apply depending on what’s being done.

In condominium buildings, there’s another layer: the corporation’s rules often focus on sound transmission. Hard flooring can be restricted or require specific underlayment to meet the building’s expectations for sound performance on upper floors. Before you install on an above-grade condo unit, review the declaration and ask the board/property manager for approval in writing, especially for hardwood and tile systems.

To verify an Ontario contractor in Kingston, use a simple checklist: (1) confirm the contractor’s provincial licensing/registration where applicable for trades involved, (2) request their certificate of liability insurance and ensure it matches your address and project dates, and (3) confirm WSIB/WCB coverage status in writing. Where offered, ask for a clearance letter or COI endorsement. Then request the proof documents before signing—reputable installers can provide these without pressure.

Best flooring for each room in your Kingston home

Choosing the best flooring in Kingston is mostly about matching the material to the room’s moisture exposure, foot traffic, and comfort needs. In living and dining areas, engineered hardwood and high-quality laminate are common favourites because they bring warmth without the same humidity sensitivity you get with solid wood. For bedrooms, homeowners often choose carpet for sound absorption and comfort, while engineered hardwood is a strong alternative if you want easier cleaning. In kitchens, luxury vinyl plank (LVP) is usually the top practical choice because spills happen and LVP is waterproof; large-format porcelain tile is the “maximum durability” option if you’re okay with the feel underfoot and the higher tile install labour.

Basements are where Kingston’s moisture realities show up. Finished basements frequently face vapour movement, occasional condensation near cold exterior walls, and humidity swings—so LVP tends to outperform solid hardwood and most laminate. Tile can work in basements too, but the cost and added weight of tile build-ups can make it more labour-intensive. Bathrooms should stick to porcelain or ceramic tile only because those rooms see direct water exposure and splash.

If you have or plan to add radiant heat, confirm radiant-compatible flooring early; it can affect underlayment selection and rules out several solid-wood setups. For a realistic cost justification: an LVP install might land around the $6.50–$10.50 per sq ft band, while tile often runs roughly $9–$20 per sq ft—so in a kitchen, spending the extra on tile makes sense if you want long-term surface durability, not just a short-term aesthetic refresh.

How to choose a flooring contractor in Kingston

Hiring the right flooring contractor in Kingston starts with verification and good documentation. Ask for proof of Ontario liability insurance and WSIB/WCB coverage—then look at the certificate details, not just the name. A current certificate of insurance should match the contractor’s legal entity and list the coverage dates for your project timeframe. For WSIB/WCB, request a clearance letter or status confirmation in writing, especially if you’re hiring for occupied homes where dust control and scheduling matter. For any associated trades (like radiant upgrades that involve electrical work), ensure they’re licensed and properly insured.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes—not one lump sum. You want a breakdown that separates materials, labour, subfloor prep, underlayment/vapour barrier, removal and disposal, transitions/stair nosing, and any levelling compounds. Then read exclusions carefully: if levelling is only “as required,” ask how they measure flatness and what qualifies as “extra.” Confirm whether permit pull (if needed for electrical or other regulated work) is included and who is responsible. Ask about warranties: workmanship warranty length, product/manufacturer warranty terms, and whether the warranty is transferable to you if you’re not the original purchaser.

On payment, avoid large upfront deposits. A common safe approach is no more than 10–15% upfront, with a holdback until substantial completion and final cleanup. Finally, require a start date and completion estimate in writing so you can plan around work schedules and occupied spaces.

Red flags in Kingston: they refuse to provide written itemised pricing, they won’t show insurance/WSIB/WCB proof, they dismiss subfloor moisture concerns without testing, they quote a “perfectly flat” result without flatness assessment, or they insist on full payment upfront before prep is complete.

Frequently asked questions — flooring in Kingston

What is the best flooring for a high-traffic area in Kingston?

For high-traffic areas in Kingston—think main hallways, entries, and living rooms—the best results usually come from materials that handle humidity swings and everyday impacts. Engineered hardwood is a strong choice because it looks like wood but tolerates movement better than solid hardwood. If you expect spills or want easier cleanup, luxury vinyl plank (LVP) with a waterproof core is often the practical winner. For budget reality, many installed LVP projects land around the $6.50–$10.50 per sq ft band, while solid hardwood installs commonly start higher (often in the low double digits per sq ft) and are best kept above grade. In older homes (over half built before 1981), make sure your contractor addresses uneven subfloors before finishing—this helps avoid squeaks and gaps later. (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census)

How do I prepare my subfloor before new flooring?

Good subfloor prep is where durable results are made, especially in Kingston’s older housing stock and basement humidity conditions. The first step is flatness: your contractor should check the subfloor with a straightedge and identify dips, ridges, and soft spots. Next comes cleaning and removal of contaminants (dust, adhesive residue, old staples that can telegraph through). If there are moisture concerns—common near exterior walls or in below-grade areas—ask for a moisture assessment and vapour control plan. For levelling, smoothing compound or patching is applied only where needed; the goal is a stable, flat base, not simply “more product.” Also clarify what’s expected of you versus the contractor in the quote. A realistic job may add $1–$4 per sq ft for subfloor repair in tougher cases, which can be the difference between a smooth install and a finish that fails early.

Is cork or bamboo flooring a good choice in Kingston?

Cork and bamboo can be good in Kingston when chosen and installed correctly, but they’re not always the easiest match for our humidity swings. Cork is often comfortable and relatively quiet, and it can work well above grade where indoor moisture stays stable. Bamboo can be attractive, but some bamboo products behave like hardwood—movement can become an issue if your home has moisture fluctuation or if you’re installing on a concrete slab that’s prone to vapour. For Kingston basements, I generally steer homeowners toward waterproof LVP or tile instead of cork or bamboo, because below-grade moisture risk is the key failure point. If you’re considering cork or bamboo, insist on manufacturer-approved underlayment, proper acclimation, and a clear plan for vapour control. Compare warranty terms closely, since many warranties are stricter about concrete and moisture conditions.

What is the difference between floating and glued-down flooring?

Floating flooring is installed without permanently bonding to the subfloor. Most click-lock laminate and many engineered hardwood options “float” over a suitable underlayment, relying on interlocking joints and a stable base. Glued-down flooring is adhered to the subfloor using the product’s approved adhesive, which can improve stability on some substrates and can reduce joint movement—but it requires proper substrate preparation and strict moisture requirements. In Kingston, floating systems can be simpler, but the subfloor still must be flat and dry. Glued-down systems are more sensitive to moisture and flatness because failures in bond can show up as lifting or gaps over time. If radiant heat is present, confirm the product and installation method are compatible. In either case, the prep work you pay for up front helps avoid expensive troubleshooting later.

What flooring holds up best to pets in a Kingston home?

For pets, durability comes from scratch resistance, easy cleaning, and spill tolerance. Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) is often the best all-around choice in Kingston homes because many waterproof LVP products resist spills and are easier to clean after accidents. It also tends to stand up better to minor surface impacts than laminate, and it avoids the moisture sensitivity of solid hardwood. Engineered hardwood can work too, especially in above-grade areas, but you still need to address humidity control and clean up promptly if your pet has an accident. If you’re comparing costs, LVP commonly lands around the $6.50–$10.50 per sq ft band installed, while engineered hardwood often sits higher (frequently up to the $12–$25 per sq ft range depending on the product and prep). Regardless of the choice, keep nails trimmed and use the right cleaning products to protect the finish.

How do I fix squeaky floors in my Kingston home?

Squeaks usually come from movement between subfloor layers, loose subfloor panels, or insufficient fasteners—not from the finish floor itself. Before adding new flooring, it’s important to identify the source by walking and testing the area (often you can hear where the squeak happens when pressure is applied). In many Kingston homes built before 1981, squeaks are common because older subfloors can loosen over time and the base wasn’t designed for modern lifestyle loads and fluctuating humidity. The fix is typically to tighten or replace fasteners and add blocking between joists or secure subfloor panels, sometimes paired with patching and levelling. If you’re planning to install carpet or LVP, ask your contractor whether they can address subfloor squeaks during prep. Doing it before installation protects your new floor and prevents the same noise from returning once everything is covered.
