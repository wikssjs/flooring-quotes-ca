Flooring options and costs in Toronto

Toronto homeowners typically have a wide range of flooring budgets, because material choice, prep work, and labour demand all move together. With 2,794,356 residents and a housing mix where 58.1% of homes were built before 1981 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), it’s common to run into uneven subfloors, older plywood layers, or moisture mitigation needs before new flooring goes down. That kind of prep is one reason the same finished look can land in very different price ranges across neighbourhoods.

Toronto’s seasonal weather brings indoor humidity swings that affect wood and some floating floors. In practice, we steer many clients toward engineered hardwood or luxury vinyl plank (LVP) because they’re more dimensionally stable for Toronto’s wet/dry cycles, and they’re far more forgiving over concrete and in below-grade situations. In colder stretches of the region—and for newer builds adding radiant floor heating—radiant-compatible products matter, and that typically rules out traditional solid hardwood in most heat systems.

Labour is also pushed up by renovation demand, so removal, levelling, and transitions (especially in older homes) can be as important as the selected material. Areas with heavy renovation activity like Etobicoke and the Downtown core (where many condos and infill homes turn over) are especially busy for installers, and booking timelines can tighten as a result.

What affects the price of flooring in Toronto

In Toronto, it’s normal to see quotes for the “same” flooring end up 30–50% apart once you account for scope and prep. The biggest drivers are Toronto’s indoor humidity swings, the moisture profile of the specific subfloor/level, and how much labour the crew needs for removal, levelling, transitions and clean-up. Even when the finished material lands in a familiar band—like laminate/vinyl in the $4–$9 per sq ft range or tile at $12–$25 per sq ft—the groundwork can shift the total dramatically.

Technically, solid hardwood expands and contracts with humidity, so it can’t be installed safely over concrete and it’s risky in basements (where vapour pressure is higher). That’s why engineered hardwood and LVP are commonly recommended in Toronto: engineered wood tolerates normal seasonal movement better, and LVP is designed for below-grade and high-humidity installs.

Older Toronto housing stock (58.1% built before 1981; Statistics Canada, 2021 Census) also means subfloors often need levelling or partial plywood repair before installation. In real jobs, that prep can add roughly $1–$4 per sq ft depending on how extensive it is. For example, a main-floor install might need feather-finish levelling in tight low spots, while a condo conversion could involve old underlay removal and subfloor smoothing.

Two concrete Toronto scenarios that change pricing: (1) if you’re replacing flooring in a split-level or basement where moisture mitigation is needed, you typically see a bigger jump when you keep moisture-tolerant products like LVP; (2) if your project includes radiant floor heating, the material selection and installation method become stricter, which can affect both labour and product choice.

Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, installing new flooring on its own usually does not require a building permit. That said, Toronto homeowners should be aware that “associated work” can trigger permit requirements—especially where electrical or structural elements are involved. For example, if your project includes in-floor heating circuit work (new wiring, modifications to controls, or changes to electrical components), electrical permits may be required. Similarly, if you’re doing structural subfloor repair beyond simple patching—like significant joist work or load-bearing adjustments—permits can become part of the discussion.

In condominiums, the rules are often more restrictive than the city or province. Many corporations control sound transmission and underlay requirements (STC/IIC considerations) when you install hard flooring on upper floors. That means you may need specific underlay materials, thickness limits, or board approval before any hardwood, engineered wood, or tile installation. Always check the condo declaration and ask for written approval in advance.

To verify a contractor properly in Toronto, follow a simple step-by-step:

  • Confirm the contractor’s Ontario business licensing status (if applicable) and request their proof of licensing details in writing.
  • Ask for a Certificate of Insurance (liability) and ensure it includes your address/project and is current.
  • Request proof of WSIB/WCB coverage (or an acceptable exemption/letter where applicable).
  • Get an itemised written scope that separates labour, materials, underlay, levelling, and disposal.
  • Verify warranty terms in writing before you pay any deposit.

Best flooring for each room in your Toronto home

Choosing flooring room-by-room is the fastest way to avoid expensive mistakes in Toronto. Our winters and humid summers create indoor humidity swings, and many older homes have subfloor variability. Because of that, living and dining spaces usually do well with engineered hardwood or high-quality laminate—both deliver warmth and visual appeal without the same level of risk as solid wood in fluctuating moisture conditions.

For bedrooms, many Toronto homeowners choose carpet for comfort and sound control, particularly on upper levels where noise matters. In master bedrooms, engineered hardwood is also a strong option when you want easy cleaning and a long service life. Kitchens and entries, however, see frequent water exposure from mopping, snow melt and spills; that’s where luxury vinyl plank (LVP) or large-format porcelain tile make the most sense. Tile is also the most reliable surface for bathrooms, because it’s meant for repeated wetting and can be sealed/maintained properly.

Basements are where Toronto owners need the most careful planning. Finished basements are subject to vapour pressure and occasional humidity spikes, so LVP is the clear winner. Solid hardwood and most laminate can struggle under those conditions. If your home has radiant floor heating (increasingly common in new custom builds), engineered hardwood, tile, and some LVP products pair better than traditional solid hardwood because heat and expansion need to be managed.

Here’s a realistic budget example: if your living room is 300 sq ft, moving from a $6–$8 per sq ft laminate to engineered hardwood at roughly $10–$16 per sq ft can be justified when you factor in Toronto’s humidity swings and the longer lifespan (often 15–35 years for engineered depending on the wear layer).

How to choose a flooring contractor in Toronto

In Toronto, a “cheap” quote often fails on the details—especially subfloor prep and moisture handling. Start by verifying Ontario coverage and credentials. Ask the contractor for: (1) liability insurance, (2) WSIB/WCB clearance or proof of coverage, and (3) a clear business identity that matches their paperwork. For insurance, you want a Certificate of Insurance showing your project/address and current dates. For WSIB/WCB, request proof (or the contractor’s status documentation) before work starts.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes. You should see labour and materials broken out separately: underlay, levelling compounds, plywood repair, removal/disposal, stair nosing, and transitions should not be hidden inside a single lump sum. Make sure the scope answers what’s included and what’s not (for example: is the moisture mitigation part of the plan, and is it priced per sq ft or as a defined allowance?). In a city with many older homes, subfloor prep is where expectations get missed—so confirm the installer is accounting for Toronto’s common unevenness.

Warranty matters too. Ask for a workmanship warranty length and whether it’s transferable if you sell your home. Product warranties should be stated with model details and the documentation you’ll receive. Payment schedules should be sensible: never pay more than about 10–15% upfront, and hold back part of the total until the job is complete and cleaned up. Finally, ask for a start date and estimated completion timeline in writing.

Red flags in Toronto: no written scope (verbal estimates only), refusing to provide insurance/WSIB proof, missing line items for levelling and transitions, vague warranty language, and asking for large upfront payments before prep is confirmed.

Frequently asked questions — flooring in Toronto

How much does flooring cost in Toronto?

In Toronto, pricing depends heavily on the flooring type plus the amount of prep your subfloor needs. As a baseline, laminate or vinyl plank often falls in the $4–$9 per sq ft range, while engineered hardwood typically runs higher (around $10–$16 per sq ft depending on the product). Tile is usually the priciest material tier at roughly $12–$25 per sq ft, and carpet installation commonly sits around $5–$11 per sq ft. For many older homes (58.1% built before 1981; Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), levelling or plywood repair is a frequent add-on, which is one reason “apples-to-apples” comparisons can differ by 30–50% across Toronto. Always compare itemised quotes that include removal, prep, transitions, and underlay.

Hardwood vs laminate vs vinyl plank — which is best?

For most Toronto homes, the best choice is the one that matches your moisture risk and maintenance tolerance. Traditional solid hardwood can look outstanding, but it’s high risk in basements and over concrete because it moves with humidity and generally isn’t installed on slab. Engineered hardwood is usually the safer “wood” option for Toronto’s indoor humidity swings, especially on above-grade floors. Laminate is typically a budget-friendly pick at about $4–$9 per sq ft, but it doesn’t forgive standing water well. Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) is often the most practical for busy households because it’s designed to handle high humidity and below-grade applications, and it commonly prices in the $4.50–$9.50 per sq ft range. If you want one product for multiple levels, LVP usually wins on reliability.

What flooring is best for a Toronto basement?

For Toronto basements, LVP is usually the best-performing option because it’s waterproof and more tolerant of the vapour and humidity that can affect below-grade spaces. Solid hardwood is not a good fit—Toronto’s basement conditions can cause seasonal movement and long-term problems, and it also doesn’t suit typical concrete subfloors. Most laminate can also swell if moisture finds its way into seams. If you’re finishing a basement, ask your contractor how they’ll address subfloor condition and any moisture mitigation plan (even a basic vapour-aware underlayment strategy can matter). In terms of budget, LVP often sits around $4.50–$9.50 per sq ft, while tile can be $12–$25 per sq ft but is also very moisture-resistant. The “best” basement floor is the one rated for below-grade with a detailed prep plan.

Can I install hardwood over concrete?

In most Toronto situations, traditional solid hardwood should not go over concrete. Solid wood typically needs an appropriate installation method that avoids direct slab moisture issues, and it’s also more vulnerable to seasonal humidity movement. If you want a hardwood look on concrete, engineered hardwood is often the more realistic path because many engineered products are designed to be installed on appropriate subfloors (including on-grade applications) using approved methods. That said, the subfloor still has to be properly prepped—flatness and moisture conditions are key. If your quote is for “hardwood over concrete,” confirm whether it’s solid or engineered, what installation method is being used (glue-down vs. approved floating system), and what moisture mitigation/level testing steps are included.

How long does flooring installation take?

Timelines in Toronto depend on floor type, prep, and the number of rooms. Floating floors (laminate and many LVP installs) can progress faster once the subfloor is level and clean, while tile and glue-down systems often take longer due to setting time and curing windows. The biggest schedule variable in older Toronto homes is subfloor prep: plywood replacement, levelling, and smoothing can add time. A typical single-level project might be done in a few working days for laminate or LVP, while tile layouts and stair work can extend the timeline. Always request a start date and completion estimate in writing from your installer, and confirm whether removal/disposal is included before they start laying. Good contractors plan for Toronto’s seasonal humidity and drying/curing needs—especially when adhesives are involved.

What flooring works with radiant heat?

In Ontario, radiant floor heating is increasingly common in new construction, and it changes flooring selection. Engineered hardwood is often a strong option because it can be installed with the right underlayment and acclimation practices. Tile is also commonly used with radiant heat because it transfers heat efficiently and handles expansion when installed correctly. Some LVP products are approved for radiant heat as well, but you must confirm compatibility with the specific manufacturer instructions. Traditional solid hardwood is usually the risky choice for radiant heating because heat and expansion can increase the likelihood of gaps or cupping if the product and installation aren’t engineered for that use. If you have radiant heat, ask your contractor to specify the exact approved flooring products and installation method (including underlay) before you sign—this is where Toronto projects most often avoid costly rework.
