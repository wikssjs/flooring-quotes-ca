Flooring options and costs in New Toronto

New Toronto homeowners have a wide range of flooring choices, but the real differences show up in how materials handle moisture and how much prep is needed. With a 2021 population of 11,463 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), the Toronto-area renovation market stays busy, and that demand typically supports steady contractor availability—though pricing can still move quickly with product shortages and labour demand.

In this part of the GTA, many homes were built in the 1960s–1990s, and those older subfloors often need levelling, plywood replacement, or moisture mitigation before you can install anything new. That prep work can add meaningfully to your project even if you choose a mid-range finish. Because of Toronto’s seasonal pattern—dry winters followed by more humid spring and summer—our most common recommendations are engineered hardwood or luxury vinyl plank. Solid hardwood looks fantastic, but it expands and contracts with humidity and it’s the riskiest choice over concrete or in basements. If your home is in areas like the Glen Agar / Lakeview corridor where basements are common and infill-style renovations are frequent, expect moisture testing and subfloor correction to come up often.

Contractors in New Toronto also price jobs based on how many trades are involved (demo, underlayment, levelling, transitions, and disposal). Labour rates tend to run higher than in many other parts of Ontario, so a “cheap” material can end up costing more after prep. What affects the price of flooring in New Toronto

In New Toronto, two quotes for the “same” flooring type can differ by roughly 30–50% once you account for subfloor condition, moisture mitigation, and the amount of finishing work (transitions, stair edging, and trim). Toronto-area homes—especially those built between the 1960s and 1990s—often require levelling, plywood replacement, or concrete moisture checks. That prep is what pushes costs up even when the material itself is in the mid band.

The technical driver we see most is Toronto’s indoor humidity swings across seasons. Solid hardwood expands and contracts with moisture and it cannot be glued or floated over concrete. It also performs poorly in basements if there’s any recurring vapour or humidity. Engineered hardwood handles humidity much better and is commonly used above grade and on-grade applications; luxury vinyl plank is even more forgiving because waterproof cores are designed for below-grade and high-humidity installs. When radiant floor heating is present—which is more common in colder-climate-minded builds and new custom homes—it tends to pair best with engineered wood, tile, and certain vinyl products, while traditional solid hardwood and some laminate setups are more limited.

Here are a few real-world examples that change the budget: if your main floor has dips that require a self-levelling pour, that can add about $1–$4 per sq ft. If you’re switching to engineered hardwood from laminate, the material step-up may be modest, but the labour can rise due to glue-down prep or stricter flatness requirements. In finished basements, a move to LVP can cost more than budget laminate, but it avoids the high-risk failure path of solid wood—so the extra $5–$12 per sq ft LVP band can actually reduce long-term rework.

All of these factors combine with higher regional renovation demand and general construction wages in the GTA, so the final number is rarely “just the floor.”

Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, installing new flooring on its own typically does not require a building permit. That means a standard install of engineered hardwood, LVP, laminate, carpet, or tile—on an existing slab or subfloor—usually falls under “finish work,” not municipal permitting. However, some related tasks can trigger permits, and this is where homeowners in New Toronto sometimes get surprised.

Work that may require permits includes: electrical permits if you’re adding or modifying radiant floor heating circuits, and any electrical work tied to controls or wiring. If you uncover structural concerns during demo—like damaged joists or subfloor sections that must be rebuilt to restore load-bearing capacity—additional approvals may be required depending on scope. Also, in condominiums, the building’s rules often matter more than permits: sound transmission requirements (often managed through STC/IIC targets) can restrict the underlay you can use, especially on upper floors.

For condos and townhomes, always review your corporation’s declaration and get written approval before installing hard surface flooring where required. For licence and coverage verification, homeowners should: (1) confirm the contractor’s Ontario licence status through the appropriate provincial online listings (where applicable to the trade scope), (2) request a current Certificate of Insurance naming you and/or the property as required, and (3) confirm WSIB/WCB coverage where applicable for the contractor and any subcontractors. Ask for proof before work starts, not after—and keep copies with your contract and receipt.

Best flooring for each room in your New Toronto home

Choosing the right flooring for each room in New Toronto comes down to moisture exposure, day-to-day traffic, and comfort under foot—especially given Ontario’s humidity swings. Living and dining spaces usually do best with engineered hardwood or a high-quality laminate: they deliver warmth and a stable look while handling seasonal changes better than solid wood. In master bedrooms, you can go either way: carpet for softness and sound control, or engineered hardwood for a cleaner, lower-allergy lifestyle.

Kitchens demand easy cleanup and spill tolerance, so luxury vinyl plank (LVP) or large-format porcelain tile are the most practical. LVP is also a favourite for entries where road salts and tracked-in moisture show up. For basements, LVP is the clear winner: it’s waterproof and designed for below-grade installs where vapour and humidity can be higher. Solid hardwood and most laminate are higher risk in basements due to movement and edge swelling if moisture conditions change.

Bathrooms should be porcelain or ceramic tile only. For homes with radiant floor heating—more common in new custom builds and in cold-weather-conscious renovations—engineered wood, tile, and certain vinyl products pair well. Traditional solid hardwood often faces expansion and heat transfer limitations, and some laminate products are restricted by manufacturer installation requirements over radiant systems.

To show how the price difference can be justified: if budget laminate runs around $4–$9 per sq ft, but your basement subfloor isn’t perfectly dry, switching to LVP at roughly $5–$12 per sq ft can cost more up front while avoiding costly rework or replacement later.

How to choose a flooring contractor in New Toronto

In New Toronto, the “best” quote is rarely the lowest line item—it’s the one that matches your subfloor reality, moisture conditions, and warranty requirements. Start by verifying Ontario coverage details. Ask for the contractor’s proof of liability insurance (and check that it’s current), and request WSIB/WCB clearance or coverage documentation where applicable. A clean Certificate of Insurance should list your property name/address as required (sometimes as an additional insured, depending on contract and condo bylaws). For trades working as part of larger projects, confirm subcontractors are covered too.

Next, get 2–3 itemised written quotes with labour and materials broken out rather than a single lump sum. You want to see separate line items for demo, subfloor levelling/repairs, underlayment, transitions/stair nosing, installation method (float vs. glue-down vs. nail-down), and disposal/hauling. Ask whether permits (if any are triggered by radiant or electrical scope) are included in the contractor’s responsibility, and whether disposal is part of the package.

Review warranty terms carefully. There should be a workmanship warranty (often several years) and separate product/manufacturer warranty details. Confirm if the workmanship warranty is transferable if you sell your home. For payment scheduling, plan to never pay more than 10–15% upfront; hold back the final balance until the job is complete, cleaned, and meets the agreed scope. Lastly, insist on a start date and a realistic completion estimate in writing so you have a clear timeline baseline.

Frequently asked questions — flooring in New Toronto

Can I install flooring over existing tile in New Toronto?

Sometimes, but it depends on the tile condition, flatness, and your new floor type. Over existing tile, the biggest concerns are lippage (raised edges), grout lines telegraphing, and whether the substrate is stable and properly bonded. For laminate, many manufacturers require a very smooth surface; if the tile is uneven, you’ll need levelling compound or a patch system. For LVP, waterproof cores can work well on many prepared substrates, but the surface still has to be flat within the product spec. If your tile is failing or loose, removing it is usually the safer route because trapping movement under new flooring can cause gaps or clicking noises. In New Toronto, we also factor in moisture: basements and slabs need checks before you commit. If you’re budgeting, know that LVP is often around $5 – $12 per sq ft in this area, but prep can change the final installed cost.

What is the best flooring for a high-traffic area in New Toronto?

For most high-traffic homes in New Toronto, luxury vinyl plank (LVP) or engineered hardwood is the practical sweet spot. LVP is waterproof and handles Ontario’s indoor humidity swings better than solid hardwood, and it tends to tolerate everyday spills and tracked moisture more confidently—especially in family zones and near kitchen/entry routes. Engineered hardwood offers a more traditional look while staying more dimensionally stable than solid wood. If you choose laminate, be selective: click-lock laminate can do well in dry zones, but it’s more vulnerable at seams if water gets underneath. For bathrooms, the answer is always tile. Price-wise, LVP commonly falls in the $5 – $12 per sq ft band, while engineered hardwood is often in the $9 – $16 per sq ft range. The “best” choice ultimately depends on whether the traffic area is near wet entries or extends into a basement-level space.

How do I prepare my subfloor before new flooring?

Good preparation is what protects your flooring warranty and prevents callbacks. First, the installer should assess flatness and firmness: older Toronto-area homes frequently need levelling and sometimes plywood replacement before installation. Next, for any basement or slab area, moisture evaluation matters because vapour can affect materials—solid hardwood is especially risky when humidity fluctuates. If the subfloor is uneven, levelling compound or underlayment may be required (exact method depends on the flooring manufacturer). Any loose sections or delaminating plywood should be repaired, not covered over. If you’re installing engineered hardwood, you’ll still want a clean, dry surface within the flatness tolerance. For LVP, a stable substrate and correct underlayment (or lack of it, depending on product) are critical. Subfloor prep commonly adds about $1–$4 per sq ft in older homes, even when the finished floor material is mid-range. In New Toronto, that’s one of the biggest reasons quotes vary significantly.

Is cork or bamboo flooring a good choice in New Toronto?

Cork can be a comfortable option because of its softness and sound absorption, but in New Toronto it’s only a good choice if you manage moisture risk correctly. Many cork products are sensitive to sustained dampness, and if you’re installing over concrete or in basement conditions, you’d need a system approved by the manufacturer (often including specific vapour barriers and underlay). Bamboo is usually more common as a solid or engineered plank; engineered bamboo may be more stable than solid, but it still needs a dry, properly prepared substrate. In our GTA market, where indoor humidity swings are common and basements are prevalent, engineered hardwood and LVP typically outperform cork/bamboo for “set it and forget it” reliability. If you love the look of cork or bamboo, ask the contractor to show you the exact product’s installation instructions for your slab/basement situation and humidity expectations. Budget-wise, most bamboo/cork offerings can land near the mid hardwood bands (similar to $9 – $16 for stable engineered options), so it’s worth double-checking fit before paying more.

What is the difference between floating and glued-down flooring?

Floating flooring means the planks/tiles are not fixed to the subfloor; they usually click together and rest on an approved underlay. Glued-down flooring uses adhesive to bond the material directly to the subfloor (or to an approved underlayment system). Floating installations are typically faster, often less sensitive to small subfloor imperfections than glue-down—but they still require a stable, flat base to avoid movement, noise, and joint stress. Glued-down installations can improve stability and reduce movement in some applications, but they require careful surface preparation and correct adhesive selection. In Ontario homes with seasonal humidity swings, engineered products can be chosen to reduce movement—yet solid hardwood remains the most moisture-sensitive and is not a good fit over concrete or in basements. When radiant floor heating is present, the recommended method depends on the product and manufacturer heat limits. If you’re comparing quotes, ask which method is being used and whether it aligns with warranty requirements.

What flooring holds up best to pets in a New Toronto home?

For pets, the strongest everyday performers in New Toronto are usually luxury vinyl plank (LVP) and porcelain/ceramic tile. LVP’s waterproof core handles spills and accidents better than laminate or carpet, and many pet-friendly LVP products have wear layers designed for claws and frequent traffic. Tile is extremely durable and cleanable, though it’s harder underfoot and can be loud unless sound solutions are used. Carpet can work if you’re willing to manage stains and keep up with cleaning, but it’s not our first pick where there’s a risk of moisture or where accidents happen—especially in basements. Engineered hardwood can be a good “looks great” option if you choose a strong wear finish and maintain humidity levels, but it still isn’t as forgiving as LVP. In budget terms, LVP is often around $5 – $12 per sq ft, while tile commonly sits in the $12 – $25 per sq ft range. Either way, pair the flooring choice with a proper underlayment/sound plan and good mat placement at entry points.
